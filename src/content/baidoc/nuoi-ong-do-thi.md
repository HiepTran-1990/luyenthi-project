In recent years, a growing number of city dwellers have taken up an unlikely hobby: keeping bees on rooftops, balconies, and in small urban gardens. What was once considered an exclusively rural practice has found an unexpected home in the heart of major cities, from New York to Paris to Tokyo.

The rise of urban beekeeping has been driven partly by growing awareness of declining bee populations worldwide. Bees play a critical role in pollinating crops and wild plants, and their decline, attributed to factors such as pesticide use, habitat loss, and disease, has raised concerns among scientists and food producers alike. Urban beekeeping enthusiasts argue that cities, somewhat counterintuitively, can offer bees a relatively safe environment, since urban gardens and parks are often less exposed to the large-scale pesticide spraying common in agricultural areas.

Beyond environmental concerns, many urban beekeepers are motivated by a desire to reconnect with nature and produce their own honey. Rooftop hives have become a feature of restaurants and hotels in several cities, with some establishments using the honey produced on-site in their menus, turning beekeeping into both a sustainability initiative and a marketing feature.

However, urban beekeeping is not without its challenges. Bees require careful management to prevent them from becoming a nuisance or safety concern to neighbours, particularly in densely populated areas. Several cities have introduced regulations specifying minimum distances between hives and public spaces, as well as requirements for beekeepers to register their hives with local authorities. Novice beekeepers are often advised to complete a training course before starting, as improperly managed hives can lead to swarming or aggressive bee behaviour.

Researchers studying urban bee colonies have made a surprising discovery: in some cases, urban bee populations appear to be healthier and more productive than their rural counterparts. This may be due to the greater diversity of flowering plants found in city gardens compared to the monoculture crops typical of large-scale agriculture, which can leave bees with limited access to varied nutrition during certain periods of the year.

Despite these promising findings, experts caution that urban beekeeping alone cannot solve the broader crisis facing global bee populations. Large-scale agricultural practices, including reduced pesticide use and the preservation of wild habitats, remain essential to addressing the issue at scale. Urban beekeeping, they suggest, should be viewed as a valuable complement to these larger efforts rather than a substitute for them.
