Over the past two decades, the fashion industry has undergone a dramatic transformation driven by a business model commonly referred to as "fast fashion." This approach prioritises speed and low cost, allowing retailers to move designs from the catwalk to store shelves within a matter of weeks rather than the months or years that traditional fashion cycles once required.

The appeal of fast fashion to consumers is straightforward: trendy clothing becomes available at prices significantly lower than those of established luxury or mid-range brands. This affordability has democratised fashion in many respects, enabling people with modest incomes to purchase clothing styles that closely resemble those seen on runways or worn by celebrities, often within days of a trend first appearing.

However, this business model carries substantial hidden costs. Producing clothing quickly and cheaply typically requires cutting corners elsewhere in the supply chain. Investigations into fast fashion manufacturing have repeatedly uncovered poor working conditions, including excessive hours and inadequate wages, particularly in factories located in countries with weaker labour protections. Workers, many of whom are young women, often bear the human cost of an industry structured to deliver garments at the lowest possible price.

Environmental concerns have also intensified alongside the growth of fast fashion. The rapid production and disposal cycle encourages consumers to view clothing as disposable rather than durable, contributing to enormous volumes of textile waste. Many garments produced under this model are worn only a handful of times before being discarded, and because they are frequently made from synthetic fibres, they can take decades or even centuries to decompose in landfills. The dyeing and treatment processes involved in textile manufacturing are also significant sources of water pollution in many producing countries.

In response to mounting criticism, some companies have introduced recycling programmes, sustainable fabric initiatives, or transparency reports detailing their supply chains. Critics, however, argue that such measures amount to only a partial solution, since the fundamental logic of fast fashion, encouraging frequent purchasing of inexpensive, quickly discarded clothing, remains largely unchanged. A genuine shift, they contend, would require consumers to buy fewer items of higher quality and companies to slow their production cycles substantially.

Meanwhile, a countertrend has begun to emerge among a segment of consumers, particularly younger shoppers, who are turning towards second-hand clothing, clothing rental services, and brands that emphasise longevity over novelty. Whether this shift represents the beginning of a lasting change in consumer behaviour, or merely a niche movement within a still-dominant fast fashion market, remains a subject of considerable debate among industry analysts.
